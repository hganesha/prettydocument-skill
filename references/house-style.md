# House style: how an essay reads

Distilled from fourteen published notes. Read it before
writing the first paragraph, and re-read it after the first draft.

## The contract with the reader

An essay is a considered argument about a system, written by someone
who has looked closely at it. The reader is intelligent, busy, and allergic to
being sold something. The tone is a senior practitioner thinking out loud —
certain where the evidence is certain, explicit where it is not.

## Structure

- **Chapters: 5–12.** Below five, it is an article. Above twelve, the argument
  has lost its spine — merge.
- **Words: 2,500–6,000 total; 250–650 per chapter.** Reading minutes are
  computed, not chosen.
- **Chapter title** — two to six words, sentence case, concrete.
  "Limits of current diagrams", "Processes and agent loops",
  "The work only you can do". Not "Chapter 3: Analysis".
- **Chapter thesis** — one sentence, arguable, specific enough to be wrong.
- **Opening chapter** — open on the concrete thing, not on the thesis or the
  essay. A scene, an artifact, a number, a diagram everyone has seen. State
  what is wrong with it in one sentence.
- **Closing chapter** — return to the opening object with what is now known.
  No summary list, no "in conclusion", no call to action.

## Paragraphs

- 40–110 words. One idea. If a second idea arrives, start a new paragraph.
- **Concrete before abstract.** Open on the thing — the file, the box, the
  number, the person — then name what it means.
- Land the claim at the end of the paragraph, not the start, when the paragraph
  is building a case.
- Vary length deliberately. Two long paragraphs in a row read as a wall; a
  one-sentence paragraph after a long one is a beat, not a style.
- One rhetorical question per chapter at most, and never at the end.

## Sentences

- Prefer the declarative. Fragments are allowed for rhythm ("None of it. Not
  one field."), but no fragment may carry a load-bearing claim.
- Cut the hedging stack: "it could perhaps be argued that this might suggest"
  → "this suggests".
- Cut the meta: "in this essay", "as we will see", "it is important to note",
  "let's dive in", "furthermore".
- Cut the filler: "very", "really", "in order to", "the fact that".
- Second person ("you") is allowed and is often right — the reader is the one
  who will meet this system. Do not mix "you" and "we" in one paragraph.
- Technical terms get one plain-language gloss the first time. After that, use
  the term.
- No emoji. No bold-for-emphasis inside prose. No exclamation marks.

## Numbers and evidence

- Every number carries a unit and a comparison: "about 1/200th of what it did
  in 2023", not "dramatically cheaper".
- Round honestly and say you rounded.
- If the source does not support a number, say what is unknown and what would
  settle it. "The public data does not measure the gap" is a stronger sentence
  than a confident estimate.
- Distinguish three registers, as Note 005 does: **validated** (directly
  checked), **inferred** (a supported interpretation), **declared** (a choice or
  intention). Name which one a claim is when it matters.

## Figures

- A figure explains structure the prose cannot. It is not a decoration and not
  a summary of the paragraph above it.
- Caption/alt text states **what to look for** and **what to distrust**: the
  scale, the source, what the figure leaves out.
- Items are noun phrases, not sentences; details are one sourced sentence.
- Never let a figure carry a claim the prose never makes.

## Words the house avoids

`leverage` (verb), `unlock`, `seamless`, `robust`, `holistic`, `ecosystem`
(unless literal), `game-changing`, `revolutionary`, `delve`, `landscape`
(unless literal), `in today's fast-moving world`, `paradigm`, `synergy`,
`best-in-class`, `cutting-edge`.

## A paragraph, for calibration

> The diagram this essay is written against. Every box is correct. None of it
> says what the system knows, what it is permitted to do, or what it did.

Three sentences. Concrete object first, then the verdict, then the three
specific absences. Nothing is hedged. No adverb does the work of a fact.

> A person sits on the left. An arrow points to a rectangle labelled AI Agent.
> Around it hover a database cylinder, a cloud, perhaps a vector store, several
> APIs and, if the architect is feeling ambitious, another rectangle labelled
> LLM. Arrows connect everything.

Then the turn:

> The problem is not PowerPoint. The problem is that our visual language has
> fallen behind the thing we are trying to describe.
